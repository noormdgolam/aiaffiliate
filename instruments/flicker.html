The Flicker

You are about to look directly at a large object while it changes, over and over, for as long as it takes — and not see it.

Look up from this screen for a moment. You have the strong impression of a detailed, stable, fully rendered world extending in every direction — not a sketch, not a summary. A picture.

This instrument tests that impression directly. Two versions of a scene will alternate, separated by a brief blank. One element differs between them. It is not small, and it is not hidden. Click it when you find it.

With the blank removed, the change is not merely easier — it is impossible to miss. It leaps. This tells you precisely what you were using to see it, and it was not a mental picture.

A change in the world produces a local motion transient: a sharp, low-level signal at one place saying something here just moved. Your attention is dragged to it automatically, before you know anything about the scene. The grey blank floods the entire field with transients at once, and the one that matters is buried among them.

Strip out that reflex and you are left with only what you were genuinely holding in mind from one moment to the next — and the answer, it turns out, is close to nothing. Perhaps four items. Perhaps fewer. Not a picture. A handful of things you happened to be attending to, plus the standing assumption that the rest is still out there behaving itself.

Why the illusion is so total

Here is the part that should unsettle you: the reason you feel you have the whole scene is that wherever you look, there it is. Every enquiry you make of your visual world is answered instantly and in full detail, because the world is right there to be re-examined, and a saccade costs you thirty milliseconds. You have mistaken permanent availability for actual possession.

O’Regan put it exactly: the world serves as its own memory. There is no reason to store a copy of something that will still be there when you check. But the fluency of the checking creates an overwhelming impression of a copy that was never made.

Subjects have great difficulty seeing large changes to a scene, even while looking directly at them, provided the change coincides with a brief global disruption. The richness of visual experience is, in this respect, an illusion. after Rensink, O’Regan & Clark, Psychological Science, 1997

Instrument I showed you a report issued about a region you never received. This one shows something worse: a report issued about the whole field, continuously, on the strength of a few sampled items and an assumption of stability. You did not notice the deficit then. You have not noticed it in the room you are sitting in now.

Both instruments have so far only interfered with your eyes. It is tempting to conclude that vision is unusually shoddy and the rest of the mind is sound. The next instrument does the same thing to your preferences.
