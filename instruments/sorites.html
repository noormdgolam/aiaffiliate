Instrument IV · Concepts

The Heap

You have used the word thousands of times without difficulty. This instrument asks you to say what it means — not in general, but here, to the grain.

Two propositions, both of which you almost certainly accept:

One. Eight hundred grains of sand in a pile is a heap.
Two. Removing a single grain from a heap always leaves a heap.

From those two, by seven hundred and ninety-nine applications of the second, one grain of sand is a heap. Something has gone wrong, and it is not the arithmetic.

Two passes. In the first, tear the pile down and stop when it is no longer a heap. In the second, build it up and stop when it becomes one. Judge by eye. Do not try to be consistent — try to be honest.

The trap, stated plainly

You hold three things that cannot all be true.

(a) Eight hundred grains is a heap. (b) One grain is not. (c) No single grain ever makes the difference.

Accept (a) and (c) and you are forced to call one grain a heap. Accept (a) and (b) and you are forced to abandon (c) — there must be some exact number n where a heap of n grains becomes a non-heap of n−1. Not approximately. Exactly. A single grain, lifted with tweezers, destroying a heap.

Everyone rejects that. And yet the alternative is that “heap” — a word you have never once been confused by — does not actually pick out anything with a boundary.

The ways out, and what each one costs

Epistemicism. There is a sharp cut-off. Some precise n is the last heap. You simply cannot know which, and neither can anyone else, because meaning is fixed by patterns of use too vast for any speaker to survey. Williamson defends this seriously. The cost is a fact about heaps that is, in principle, unknowable to every competent user of the word.

Degrees. “Heap” is not true or false but true-to-a-degree, sliding from 1 to 0 across the range. The cost: you have replaced one sharp boundary with two, and now owe an exact account of where degree 0.9 becomes degree 0.89.

Nihilism. There are no heaps. The predicate is incoherent and picks out nothing. The cost is obvious: you were looking at one.

No option is free. The argument is 2,300 years old and it is not solved.

Why this instrument is here

Because “heap” is not the interesting case. The interesting case is you.

A fertilised cell is not a person; you are. Somewhere between, on the same kind of gradient, without any single cell division that could plausibly be the moment. A person with advancing dementia does not stop being themselves on a Tuesday. Your body has replaced most of its material several times over and you have never noticed a discontinuity. The neural pattern that composes your memories and character today differs from the one twenty years ago by more than a heap differs from a heap minus a hundred grains.

If “heap” cannot survive one grain of pressure, ask what “the same person” is doing carrying the weight it carries — in your promises, your guilt about things done decades ago, your fear of your own death.

Identity is not a relation that either holds absolutely or fails absolutely. What matters in survival comes in degrees, and it can hold to a greater or lesser extent between me and a future person. after Derek Parfit, Reasons and Persons, 1984
