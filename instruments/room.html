The Room

The previous six instruments found errors in you. This one will find none. You will perform flawlessly, and that is the problem.

You are sealed in a room. Messages arrive through a slot in a script you have never seen. You have a book of rules for manipulating the marks — nothing else. No dictionary, no translation, no hint that the marks mean anything at all.

Searle’s room

John Searle put this argument in 1980 against the claim that a computer running the right program would thereby understand. Grant the program everything: it answers in flawless Chinese, indistinguishable from a native speaker, passing any test you like. Now be the one running it. You have the rule book, you manipulate the symbols, the answers come out perfect — and you do not understand a word of Chinese. Syntax is not sufficient for semantics. Running the program cannot be what understanding is, because you just ran it and nothing was understood.

You have now been in the room. It is not a thought experiment you were told about; you did the work, and the transcript above is yours. Whatever produced those four impeccable answers, it was not comprehension. There was none in the room to draw on.

The reply that will not go away

The Systems Reply: of course you don’t understand — you are one component. The claim was never that the CPU understands. The claim is that the whole system does: you, plus the book, plus the papers, plus the procedure. Asking the person inside whether they understand is like asking a neuron whether it speaks English.

Searle’s answer: then internalise the lot. Memorise the book, do the arithmetic in your head, work in an open field. There is now no system apart from you — and you still do not understand Chinese. Critics respond that a person who has genuinely internalised a complete Chinese-speaking program has arguably instantiated a second competence they cannot introspect, and that Searle’s appeal to “I would still know I don’t understand” simply assumes the point at issue.

Forty-five years on, this is unresolved. Do not let anyone tell you the argument obviously succeeds, and do not let anyone tell you it was obviously refuted.

Turn it around

Here is the move that makes the room genuinely dangerous, and it is why this instrument is last.

Your neurons do not understand English. Not one of them. Each is a small electrochemical device that sums inputs and fires or does not, according to rules as blind and local as anything in that book. There is no cell in your head that grasps the meaning of the word meaning. And yet — you claim — the assembly understands.

So the room proves either less or more than it appears. Either symbol manipulation can constitute understanding when it is organised in the right way, in which case the man in the room was simply the wrong place to look. Or it cannot, in which case the same argument, aimed at your skull, says nothing in there understands either. You cannot keep Searle’s conclusion and your own comprehension without saying what the difference is — and “mine is made of neurons” is not yet an answer, it is a location.

I have inputs and outputs that are indistinguishable from those of the native Chinese speaker, and I can have any formal program you like, but I still understand nothing. John Searle, “Minds, Brains, and Programs”, 1980

Notice, finally, what you cannot do. You cannot check. You are certain you understand these sentences, and that certainty is a report issued by the same apparatus that told you the visual field was complete, the scene was in memory, the preference was yours, the boundary was there, and the decision was timed. Every one of those reports arrived with exactly this confidence.

That is the last instrument. The ledger is waiting.
