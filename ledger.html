What you have just read

It is a portrait of someone, in the second person, in confident prose, assembled from behavioural traces by a few hundred lines of JavaScript that has never met you and cannot in principle know what anything is like for you.

It is not nothing. It is genuinely about you — those are your measurements, and a stranger reading it would learn something true. But you know exactly what it is, because you watched it being made: a model, built from outside, from evidence, for the purpose of making a person’s behaviour tractable and predictable.

Now the question the whole apparatus has been walking toward.

What makes you so sure the other one is different?

You have a second portrait. The one you carry — a rich, continuous sense of being a particular person, with a history, a character, motives you can inspect, decisions you make, and a definite location just behind the eyes.

Seven times now, that portrait has been caught in the act of construction.

It reported a complete visual field it never received. It reported a scene it was not holding. It reported reasons for a choice that was not made. It reported a boundary it drew twice in different places. It reported the time of a decision it could not have timed. It reported values that moved when the sentence moved. And it reported perfect understanding of marks that meant nothing to it at all.

Every one of those reports was sincere. Every one arrived with the same immediate, unearned confidence as the sentence you are reading now. And there is no eighth faculty standing behind them, checking.

For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other — of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception. David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, 1739

Hume went looking for the self and found only the contents. Two and a half centuries later the same conclusion arrives from the other direction: Metzinger’s claim is that the brain runs a self-model, and that its crucial property is transparency — you cannot see it as a model. You do not experience a representation of a self. You experience a self, because the representation is doing its job so well that the machinery is invisible. A window you can see through perfectly is a window you cannot see.

Dennett puts it as a centre of narrative gravity: not a thing in the head, but an abstraction the brain posits to organise an enormous quantity of parallel activity into one coherent story — real in the way a centre of gravity is real. There is no particle of centre-of-gravity in a chair, and it is not a fiction either.

What this does not show

Be careful here, because there is a cheap nihilism available and it is not what follows.

None of the seven instruments shows that nobody is home. They do not touch the fact that there is something it is like to be you — that fact survived every procedure intact, and is arguably the one thing on this site that cannot be got at experimentally at all. Nor do they show that your choices are not yours, or that your values are worthless, or that reasoning is futile. You reasoned your way through all seven, and the reasoning worked.

What they show is narrower and much harder to put down: your sense of direct, privileged, immediate access to your own mind is not a channel. It is a construction, of the same kind and the same reliability as your perception of the outside world — and you have no way, from the inside, to tell a good one from a bad one.

Which is not a counsel of despair. It is the ordinary condition of every instrument that has ever been used to measure anything: it must be checked against something other than itself. That is what evidence is for, what other people are for, what argument is for. You are not in a worse position than a thermometer. You are simply in the same one, and the news is that you were never in a better one.
