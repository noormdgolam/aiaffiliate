Instrument III · Introspection

The Preference

Of everything you are sure about, your own preferences ought to be the safest. You are not reporting on the world here. You are reporting on yourself.

You will be shown pairs of forms and asked which you prefer. There is no correct answer; they are meaningless shapes generated a moment ago. After each choice you will be asked to say briefly what drew you to it.

This instrument does something to you during the procedure. You will be told exactly what, in full, the moment it ends — and nothing is transmitted anywhere. Saying more now would destroy the measurement.

Answer quickly and honestly. Do not deliberate; there is nothing to deliberate about.

On two of the five trials, the instrument showed you the form you rejected and told you it was your choice. Here is the record.

What the sentence was actually made of

If you wrote a reason for a form you had just turned down, notice what did not happen. You did not experience confusion, or a blank, or a sense of retrieving nothing. A reason arrived — fluently, in the first person, with the ordinary feeling of remembering why.

That reason cannot have been a memory of your deliberation, because the deliberation went the other way. It was constructed on the spot, from the form in front of you, and then handed to you already labelled as introspection. The label is the whole problem: the manufactured explanation and the genuine one are delivered in exactly the same voice, and you have no channel that distinguishes them.

This is not a quirk of meaningless shapes. Johansson and colleagues ran it with photographs of faces and with jam and tea in a supermarket, and roughly three-quarters of substitutions went undetected — with subjects going on to defend the swapped choice, sometimes citing the specific feature that had been the reason they rejected it.

The subjects not only failed to notice the change, they also proceeded to give confabulatory reasons for a choice they never made. Johansson, Hall, Sikström & Olsson, Science, 2005

The older result underneath it

Nisbett and Wilson found the same architecture in 1977: people asked why they preferred one item in a row of identical items produced confident reasons — texture, quality, weave — when the only real predictor was position. Told the true cause, they rejected it. Not defensively. They simply could not find it by looking inward, because that is not something looking inward can find.

Set the three findings side by side. Instrument I: a report about a region you never received. Instrument II: a report about a scene you were not holding. Instrument III: a report about a decision you did not make. In every case the report was fluent, immediate, and sincere.

Sincerity is not the issue. You were not lying. There is no liar here — that is precisely what makes it hard to defend against. The system that produces your self-explanations is not the system that makes your choices, and it has no privileged access to it. It is a press office, working from the same public evidence as everyone else, but issuing statements in the first person.

The next instrument leaves your mind alone and goes after your concepts.
