Instrument I · Perception

The Blind Spot

There is a hole in the middle of your sight, roughly the size of nine full moons laid side by side. You have never seen it. This instrument will show you what is not there.

Where the optic nerve leaves each eye, the retina has no photoreceptors. That patch of the world is not dim, or blurred, or grey. It is simply not received. And yet you have never once noticed a gap.

The obvious explanation — that the other eye covers it — is wrong, and you can prove it wrong in about forty seconds. Close one eye and the gap does not appear. Something else is happening.

Apparatus I — fixation array Stage 1 / 2 · locate
1. Sit about an arm’s length from the screen.
2. Cover your left eye. Do not squint — cover it.
3. With your right eye, stare at the ✛ and do not look away.
4. Keeping your gaze on the ✛, drag the slider slowly. At some point the disc on the right will vanish completely.

Now the interesting part

You have located the hole. Hold everything exactly as it was — same eye covered, same distance, same separation. The instrument has changed the field to a ruled pattern, and has cut a disc out of it at precisely the position where your blind spot sits.

There is a hole in the image. Look at the ✛ again and tell the instrument what you see where the hole is.

Every claim your visual experience makes about that patch of the world is manufactured. No light from it reached you. The lines you saw continue across the gap were not in the image on your screen, and they were not in the light entering your eye. They were supplied.

Notice what this rules out. Vision cannot be a matter of the world arriving in you and being inspected. If it were, the gap would show up as a gap — a dark hole, an absence, a smear. Instead the missing region is delivered to you already finished, seamless, and confident, in a form indistinguishable from the parts you actually received.

Two readings, and neither one is comfortable

The first: the brain genuinely paints. Ramachandran and Gregory showed in 1991 that a patch of visual noise surrounding a blind-spot gap gets completed as noise, with the right texture and grain — which looks a great deal like active rendering.

The second, Dennett’s: nothing is painted, because there is no canvas and no one to look at it. The brain simply never receives a report of an absence, so it never issues one. You do not see a filled-in gap; you fail to see that anything is missing, which from the inside is identical.

The two readings disagree about the mechanism and agree about the thing that matters here: the seamlessness of your visual world is not evidence that your visual world is complete. It is evidence that you are not equipped to detect its incompleteness. Absence of the signal is not delivered to you as a signal of absence.

The brain does not need to “fill in” for the simple reason that the region is not being reported as empty — and nothing in the machinery is asking. after D. C. Dennett, Consciousness Explained, 1991

Hold on to this, because the next six instruments are all versions of it. Each one takes some faculty you would swear is reporting the truth — your memory of a scene, your knowledge of your own preferences, the boundaries of your concepts, the moment you decided, your values, your understanding — and finds the same architecture underneath: a confident report, issued by a system that has no way of telling you what it doesn’t have.

You just watched your visual system do it. You could not feel it happening. That is the whole problem in one image.
